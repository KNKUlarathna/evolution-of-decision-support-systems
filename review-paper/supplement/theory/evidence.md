# Organizational theory evidence extraction

Date: 21 October 2026. Purpose: assess an explanatory review of DSS evolution as a **conditional** feedback between organizational complexity, data growth, information-processing demand, and support architectures. Four theory sources have accessible full text; a fifth historical critique remains abstract-only and is not recommended as a substantive full-text source.

The three scanned PDFs were rasterized at 130 dpi and locally OCRed with Tesseract; original printed page labels were used for locations. OCR can misread diagrams or names, so passages were interpreted in context. Simon’s supplied PDF already has OCR. Sources are theories/syntheses with examples and cited studies; none is a longitudinal empirical test of the entire proposed feedback loop. Public availability does not establish permission to redistribute article PDFs; open licenses were not established.

## GALBRAITH1974 — demand/capacity fit with alternatives to data expansion

**Metadata:** J. R. Galbraith, “Organization Design: An Information Processing View,” *Interfaces*, vol.4, no.3, pp.28–36, 1974, DOI10.1287/inte.4.3.28. Contemporary metadata sometimes reports the journal’s later name; cite the original journal title visible on the article.

- **p.28, The Information Processing Model:** the central proposition links greater task uncertainty with more information that decision makers must process *during task execution* to maintain a given performance level. More predictable tasks permit preplanning; new knowledge during execution changes allocations, schedules and priorities. This supports the arrow **task uncertainty/interdependence → information-processing requirements**, not the blanket equation “bigger organization = more bytes.”
- **p.28–29, Mechanistic Model; rules/hierarchy/goals:** division into specialist subtasks creates a coordination requirement because the efficacy of one action depends on others. Unanticipated exceptions can overload hierarchical referral. Product/market/technology changes can invalidate subgoals. This gives a concrete organizational mechanism for rising complexity.
- **pp.29–31, Design Strategies:** four responses are discussed: slack resources; self-contained tasks; vertical information systems; lateral relations. The first two reduce processing requirements; the latter two increase capacity. Slack buffers or reduced interdependence therefore form a **balancing loop**, with costs in performance, specialization or resources. More sophisticated DSS is not the only response.
- **p.32, Investment in Vertical Information Systems:** more frequent replanning requires more clerical work, computer time and input/output resources. Formalizing language can compress transmission. More frequent information provision may overload decision makers, requiring attention to the capacity of the receiver and human–machine arrangements. Vertical systems suit formalizable/quantifiable data; qualitative/ambiguous information may favor moving decisions toward where information resides. This is a strong historical link between organizational coordination, information frequency, computing resources and architecture.
- **p.36, Choice of Strategy:** relative cost and context determine the strategy; combinations are possible. Organizations may also act on their environments through contracts/coalitions to reduce uncertainty. Capacity mismatch can manifest as budget/schedule overruns rather than innovation.

**Boundary:** Galbraith supplies an already established information-processing perspective. Do not claim that complexity-demand-capacity fit is newly discovered by the review. His model does not measure physical data-volume trends, show a universal DSS sequence, or empirically establish “DSS enables expansion which creates complexity.” The reverse expansion arrow needs separate evidence or must be labeled a synthesis proposition.

## TUSHMAN1978 — data is not information; contingent design is not deterministic

**Metadata:** M.L. Tushman and D.A. Nadler, “Information Processing as an Integrating Concept in Organizational Design,” *Academy of Management Review*, vol.3, no.3, pp.613–624, 1978. AOM DOI10.5465/amr.1978.4305791; JSTOR DOI10.2307/257550 identifies the same work. Some harvested metadata truncates the last page to613; use the article’s full page range.

- **p.614, introduction/working assumptions:** information processing includes gathering, interpreting and synthesizing information for organizational decisions. The authors distinguish data from information: relevance, accuracy, timeliness and concision matter; data processing need not change knowledge. Plans, budgets, performance feedback, inventories and market conditions are included, rather than equating information with stored digital records.
- **pp.615–616, Proposition1:** subunit task characteristics, task environment and inter-unit task interdependence generate uncertainty. More reciprocal/complex interdependence raises coordination and mutual problem-solving demands. This supports operationalizing complexity with task variation/interdependence/environmental change rather than headcount alone.
- **pp.616–617, Propositions2–3:** rising work uncertainty raises information-processing requirements, while structures differ in their capacity to process appropriate information accurately and promptly. Capacity is therefore an organizational property as well as computing throughput.
- **p.621, Proposition4A:** alternative formal and lateral mechanisms mean there is no unique one-to-one mapping from requirements to structure. The choice depends on the nature of data and organizational conditions/values. Simpler mechanisms should be used until residual interdependence justifies costlier integrating arrangements.
- **p.621, Proposition5:** effective units adapt structure to changed requirements over time; different subunits, or the same subunit at different phases, may need different structures. The authors discuss cited research, but this remains an integrating theory/review rather than their own longitudinal DSS adoption dataset.

**Boundary:** supports contingency and adaptation, not a deterministic technological ladder or proof that all organizations face monotonically rising complexity. A decline in uncertainty can justify more routine/mechanistic arrangements. Do not count this paper as independent primary empirical confirmation of every cited historical study.

## DAFT1986 — the crucial counterargument to “more data fixes complexity”

**Metadata:** R.L. Daft and R.H. Lengel, “Organizational Information Requirements, Media Richness and Structural Design,” *Management Science*, vol.32, no.5, pp.554–571, 1986, DOI10.1287/mnsc.32.5.554.

- **pp.554–555, introduction:** information is processed to reduce both uncertainty and equivocality. The paper reviews studies associating task variety or interdependence with communication needs, while distinguishing these from ambiguity. New data can create confusion rather than settle interpretation.
- **pp.555–556, assumptions:** organizational processing must coordinate diverse departmental activities and bridge differing goals/interpretations. Information sharing and coalition formation distinguish organization-level processing from isolated individual cognition. Technology, interdepartmental coordination and environmental demands jointly matter.
- **pp.556–557, Two Information Contingencies:** uncertainty concerns missing answers to sufficiently specified questions; equivocality concerns ambiguity and conflicting interpretations about what the situation/questions mean. Under high equivocality, extra factual records alone may not solve the decision problem. Shared interpretation and exchange of perspectives are needed.
- **p.557, Figure1:** high/low uncertainty crossed with high/low equivocality yields different information requirements. High uncertainty/low equivocality supports gathering explicit data; high equivocality instead requires problem framing, shared language and opinion exchange. Both may coexist.
- **p.568, discussion:** the authors explicitly criticize counting communications/data flow as sufficient for understanding organizational information processing; the amount and richness of information both matter. This limits “data volume” as a proxy for organizational demand.
- **pp.568–569:** they identify their frameworks as propositions requiring further research/testing. This is theoretical synthesis with reviewed evidence, not direct experimental proof of an optimal DSS design.

**Boundary:** do not infer that a richer technological interface automatically resolves ambiguity or value conflict. “Media richness” here concerns reducing equivocality; it is not synonymous with multimedia size or model parameter count. Do not merge this paper’s uncertainty notion with statistical predictive uncertainty without explanation.

## SIMON1971 — attention as the scarce resource and filtering as balancing response

**Metadata:** H.A. Simon, “Designing Organizations for an Information-Rich World,” in *Computers, Communications, and the Public Interest*, M. Greenberger, Ed. Baltimore, MD: The Johns Hopkins Press, 1971, pp.37–72. The scanned chapter includes panel discussion; only Simon’s identified speaker contribution is used for claims below.

- **pp.40–41, The Scarcity of Attention:** abundant information consumes scarce recipient attention. Information-processing capacity cannot be represented adequately by counting bits alone, since encoding changes bit counts; recipient time is a useful attention-oriented perspective.
- **pp.42–44, Information Overload:** the chapter questions treating overload as automatically requiring more hardware. The attention-saving test asks whether a processing system reduces demands on other systems; attention-intensive outputs can negate technical gains.
- **p.44, intelligence/management systems:** the aim is to buffer recipients from excessive information through decisions about collection, condensation, indexing, storage, access and communication. The example of replacing teletypes with faster line printers illustrates a capacity expansion that ignores downstream processing limits. These are examples in a conceptual argument, not new controlled studies.
- **pp.45–46, The Need to Know:** access, models and compact theories can substitute for stockpiling facts. Deduplication, compression and on-demand derivation can reduce stored information without necessarily reducing useful knowledge. Data-analysis capability should keep pace with storage propensity.

**Boundary:** this supports **data/communication growth → attention strain → selection/filtering/design response** and a balancing loop. It does not imply that all data growth is harmful or that scientific models fully eliminate storage needs. Do not attribute moderator Daddario’s comments on p.38 or other discussants’ later statements to Simon.

## ACKOFF1967 — abstract-only reserve, not a fully assessed source

The publisher abstract challenges assumptions that managers mainly lack relevant information, need all information they request, or necessarily make better decisions once supplied with it, and proposes embedding MIS in a control system. It is useful historical context for avoiding technological determinism. No full article was obtained in this task, so do not infer the content/effectiveness of its detailed procedure or example. Prefer the four assessed full-text sources for substantive claims, or explicitly mark Ackoff as abstract-only.

## Proposed operational definitions for the rebuilt review

1. **Organizational complexity:** interdependence among differentiated tasks/subunits, task variety, and uncertainty of coordination/environment. These are distinguishable dimensions, not a single observed universal index. Regulatory/stakeholder complexity requires additional sources.
2. **Data volume:** quantity of recorded observations/transactions/documents generated or available per unit time. More bytes can reflect encoding/duplication and need not be more information. Variety, velocity, quality and access are separate modifiers.
3. **Processing demand:** work needed to acquire, validate, interpret, coordinate and act on decision-relevant information. It can rise without increased recorded volume, particularly under equivocality.
4. **Effective capacity:** the combined technical and organizational ability to convert relevant evidence into timely coordinated action, subject to attention, semantics, decision authority and implementation constraints.

## Four candidate propositions (review synthesis, not established empirical findings)

- **P1—Conditional demand:** increasing task interdependence and environmental uncertainty increases information-processing requirements when existing rules/plans cannot absorb the additional exceptions. (Strong theoretical support: Galbraith28–29; Tushman615–617.) Organizational size or stored bytes alone do not establish this condition.
- **P2—Capacity-fit pressure:** when requirements exceed effective capacity, organizations may expand DSS/integration capacity, redesign coordination, reduce interdependence, accept slack, or lower performance. A unique next-generation architecture is not inevitable. (Galbraith29–36; Tushman621.)
- **P3—Ambiguity boundary:** increasing relevant data can reduce missing-fact uncertainty, but cannot by itself resolve ambiguity about goals or interpretations; shared sensemaking/negotiation remains necessary. (Daft556–557,568.)
- **P4—Attention balancing loop:** cheaper collection/transmission can increase outputs faster than recipient capacity, so filtering, aggregation and selective collection may improve decisions while reducing information flow. (Simon40–46; Galbraith32.)

**Feedback closure still requiring other evidence:** the reverse claim that DSS-enabled organizational expansion creates new interdependencies and additional data, closing a reinforcing loop, is not directly established by these four works. It should use separate empirical digital-organizing studies or be explicitly presented as a conditional conjecture. These sources justify demand/capacity fit and balancing responses, not an inevitable self-reinforcing coevolution law.
