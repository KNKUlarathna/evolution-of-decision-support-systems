# Historical DSS evidence for the organizational-complexity/data feedback review

Assessed 21 October 2026. Eight full-text works are included. “Full text” means available complete text with the indicated relevant sections assessed; it does not imply every sentence has been audited. Published-page references are given where reliable; Internet Archive scans contain blanks, so scan PDF page numbers are used for those working papers. These are source-specific mechanisms, not proof of one universal evolutionary law. No new empirical measurements were made.

## 1. little1969 — primary model-driven decision support

**Version:** John D. C. Little, *Models and Managers: The Concept of a Decision Calculus*, MIT Sloan Working Paper 403-69, June 1969. Public MIT Libraries scan through Internet Archive; this is not the separately published 1970 journal version.

**Located evidence:** Abstract, scan p.9, states that models should be simple, robust, controllable, adaptive, sufficiently complete and easy to communicate with; ADBUDG II is in trial use by three product managers. Sections 1–2, scan pp.11–15, explain the manager/model interface problem. In particular, p.15 describes model builders responding to rejection by adding complexity, thereby worsening comprehensibility. Section 3, scan pp.17–21, describes iterative refinery-scheduling/model-use anecdotes and the use of sensitivity analysis to uncover input errors and update managerial intuition. Section 4, scan p.23 onward, argues for resisting detail until users can use simpler models. Section 5, scan p.29 onward, presents ADBUDG II and distinguishes a single accountable product manager from elaborate coordination in complex processes.

**Mechanism usable in synthesis:** Greater computational/model sophistication does not imply use or better decisions. Iteration can expose missing information, lead to new inputs, and educate judgment; the interface and controllability regulate this loop. Complexity can also feed on itself when technical designers add detail in response to mistrust. These are plausible balancing/amplifying pathways present at the beginning of DSS history, not discoveries of modern AI.

**Evidence class/context:** Conceptual/design essay, practitioner anecdotes and small prototype trial. Do not treat three trial managers as a controlled organizational outcome study, or cite any universal accuracy/adoption effect. Do not assert that model complexity is the same as organizational complexity.

## 2. gorry1971 — primary contingency of decision and information requirements

**Version:** G. Anthony Gorry and Michael S. Scott Morton, *A Framework for Management Information Systems*, MIT Sloan Working Paper 510-71, February 1971. The cover identifies it as a revision of Working Paper 458-70.

**Located evidence:** Introduction, scan p.7, observes broad growth in computer/MIS activity during 1955–1970 without proportionate effects on managerial decisions. Scan p.9 names two enabling developments: remote access/short turnaround/flexible interfaces, and conceptual understanding of organizational decision processes and models. Scan p.11 expressly says the framework is a static snapshot, not a process model of systems implementation. Framework discussion, scan pp.13–21, distinguishes strategic planning, management control and operational control. Scan p.19 says information requirements differ in fundamental character, not merely aggregation. Scan p.25 argues that management/strategic work need not consume masses of operational data; sampling can be more cost effective, and direct coupling can be expensive and unnecessary. Scan p.29 differentiates structured and unstructured problem solving.

**Mechanism usable in synthesis:** Organizational differentiation changes the kinds of information and support required; demand cannot be represented simply by the number of bytes. Technology and conceptual architecture co-enable interactive support. Selective acquisition, abstraction and sampling can reduce the data burden even where managerial problems are difficult.

**Critical implication for the proposed feedback loop:** The C→D link must be conditional. More organizational complexity does not necessarily require more detailed data or larger operational databases. A DSS may reduce required information through a well-chosen model or representation.

**Evidence class/context:** Foundational conceptual framework informed by contemporary practice. Not a longitudinal historical test and not an empirical estimate of feedback gains.

## 3. shim2002 — broadening decision scope and networked organization

**Located evidence:** Journal pp.111–113 describe expansion from individual to group/organizational support, the database/model/interface combination, and infrastructure eras. Page113 describes flexible information needs and modeling for semi-structured decisions and forecasts more global, complex and connected environments with inter-organizational supply chains. Page116 links e-commerce/CRM to expected demand for customer-transaction analysis. Pages117–118 discuss partly overlapping group goals, communication and coordination, time/space constraints, and limits created by reduced communication cues in virtual teams. Section4.3 also reports a lack of sustained project-team evidence in the reviewed literature.

**Mechanism usable in synthesis:** Network reach and broad access enable wider collaboration, while wider collaboration introduces coordination/interpretation requirements. This supports a historically articulated coevolutionary mechanism, but the p.113/p.116 statements are forecasts in 2002, not observed 21st-century longitudinal effects. Separate retrospective descriptions, forecasts and the present review’s interpretation.

**Evidence class/context:** Historical/technical narrative review based on an invited panel, not a causal adoption study. Its technological eras should not be converted into an inevitable replacement sequence. Do not reuse its quantitative group/meeting claims as new evidence without checking underlying originals.

## 4. power2004 — taxonomy prevents mixing analytical levels

**Located evidence:** Abstract and pp.158–160 define one primary dimension (dominant DSS technology) and three secondary dimensions (targeted users, purpose and deployment/enabling technology). The five generic types are communications-, data-, document-, knowledge- and model-driven. Framework exposition, pp.160–164, explains these dimensions and examples.

**Mechanism usable in synthesis:** A model-driven tool can be deployed on the Web; group scope is not equivalent to a model architecture; warehouses, knowledge components and interfaces can coexist. Use configuration families organized by the information-processing problem, and record deployment and organizational scope separately. This is an antidote to the earlier manuscript’s table mixing algorithms, workflows and organizational arrangements as equivalent generations.

**Evidence class/context:** Conceptual classification. Supports distinctions and analytical discipline, not an empirical sequence or growth-rate claim.

## 5. arnott2008 — empirical review of research/practice disconnect

**Located evidence:** pp.657–659, methods: content analysis of 1093 DSS articles in 14 journals, 1990–2004; categories and calibration explained. Table2 and discussion pp.660–661 report only10.1% high/very-high practical relevance under the authors’ subjective coding; enterprise reporting/analysis and warehousing have stronger relevance yet represent only8.6% of the sampled literature. Table4, pp.661–662, classifies empirical and nonempirical research. Section3.3, p.662, says merely mentioning judgment/decision theory differs from integrally using it;47.8% did not integrally cite it under their coding scheme.

**Mechanism usable in synthesis:** Research attention is not a valid proxy for practical prevalence or evolution. A review should avoid inferring organizational adoption from recent AI publication volume. The distinction between theoretical/benchmark findings and organization practice is an established DSS methodological concern.

**Evidence class/context:** Empirical content analysis of research publications, not observation of organizations. The relevance figures are coded judgments bounded to this journal/time sample. Use them only with that denominator and methodological qualification, not as present-day DSS effectiveness statistics.

## 6. chaudhuri1997 — concrete data-integration feedback tension

**Located evidence:** PDF p.1 / journal p.65 contrasts OLTP and OLAP. Operational transactions read/update tens of records; analytical queries may scan millions. The text describes operational databases as hundreds of MB to GB and projects enterprise warehouses at hundreds of GB to TB. These are1997 descriptions/projections, not current benchmarks. PDF p.2 / p.66 explains enterprise-wide integration and departmental data marts: marts enable faster rollout without organization-wide consensus but can create long-run integration problems if a complete business model is absent. PDF pp.2–3 describe extraction, cleaning, integration, refreshing, metadata repositories, decentralized/federated versus integrated architectures, and data management tasks.

**Mechanism usable in synthesis:** More functional data sources and cross-functional analytical needs require integration; a warehouse adds processing capability by separating workloads and representing common dimensions. Local acceleration through departmental marts can defer consensus costs and later amplify integration complexity. Thus D/S (data scope/system scope) can increase governance/coordination work even when query technology is effective.

**Countermechanism:** Integration and common metadata can compress/reduce coordination problems. Architecture may stabilize the loop rather than always increase complexity.

**Evidence class/context:** Technical tutorial/review with examples and design trade-offs, not controlled proof that warehouses increase organizational complexity. Distinguish an architectural rationale from a measured organizational outcome.

## 7. fayyad1996 — volume-to-interpretation pressure, not “more data equals knowledge”

**Located evidence:** pp.37–38 explain why growing data overwhelms manual analysis and motivate KDD. p.38 discusses scale and bottlenecks. Application examples pp.38–40 include scientific image classification and business contexts; these are illustrative reports, not comparable effect-size estimates. The KDD definition and multistep process around pp.40–42 include domain understanding, selection, cleaning/preprocessing, transformation, mining, interpretation/evaluation and possible iteration, rather than treating an algorithm as the complete system.

**Mechanism usable in synthesis:** Increased collected data makes manual interpretation costly and motivates computational pattern discovery; useful knowledge depends on a larger human/organizational process. Iteration may revise goals, data selection, transformations or representations. This supports a data-to-information-processing-demand link and a balancing role for selection/abstraction.

**Evidence class/context:** Technical/conceptual overview with application examples. It does not estimate how rising organizational complexity caused data growth, nor show a universally positive feedback loop.

## 8. feigenbaum1977 — knowledge engineering as an alternative resource

**Located evidence:** pp.1014–1015 introduce PUFF, expert elicitation, rules and pulmonary interpretation. Page1015 reports150 validation cases and approximately90–100% agreement under the described disease/severity matching conditions. These are agreement results from an early project description; not a measure of organizational benefit. pp.1016–1017 discuss knowledge engineering and expert knowledge, as distinct from merely generic search.

**Mechanism usable in synthesis:** Reusable expert representations can augment information-processing capacity without relying primarily on ever-larger datasets. Expertise elicitation and maintenance are resources and possible bottlenecks. Do not collapse the move to knowledge-based support into an unqualified response to increasing data volume.

**Evidence class/context:** Invited synthesis and project case descriptions. Not a long-run organization study. It supplies a rival/parallel route: scarce expertise and interpretive requirements, not just volume, can motivate DSS change.

## Cross-source conditional propositions (review synthesis, not established empirical laws)

1. Greater task/inter-unit differentiation increases information-processing requirements when it creates interdependence, uncertainty or contested meaning; required *type/variety* of information may change without an increase in detailed-data volume (gorry1971; theory work to be integrated by parent).
2. Growing collected data can exceed human interpretation capacity and motivate new selection, integration or analytical configurations (chaudhuri1997; fayyad1996). Capacity is a necessary affordance in some workloads, not sufficient evidence of decision quality (little1969; gorry1971).
3. Extending shared support across units may reduce local processing/communication costs while increasing integration and coordination obligations (chaudhuri1997; shim2002). Direct evidence of organization-wide growth effects is limited in these sources; label the reverse-feedback branch as conditional/proposed.
4. Abstraction, sampling, understandable models and shared semantics can reduce effective complexity or data requirements, creating balancing loops (little1969; gorry1971; chaudhuri1997).
5. Organizational judgment, values and accountability are not interchangeable with algorithmic capacity. Changes in technology publication trends do not demonstrate organizational DSS transitions (arnott2008; power2004).
